Dino by Ucademy

Searching for product-market fit in a new line of business

A gamified study app for secondary school students, launched as an MVP to find product-market fit.

Timeline
May – Nov 2025
Role
Senior Product Designer
Team
CEO, instructional designer, engineer
Platform
iOS and Android

What it was

Dino was a gamified mobile app to help secondary school students study on their own, launched by Ucademy as a new line of business. We started with the final year of high school, where Ucademy already had content and teachers. The MVP had one question to answer: could students build a study habit on their own?

I was the only designer on a four-person team, with the CEO as project lead, an instructional designer and an engineer, plus an external advisor we consulted at key moments.

What I did

From May to November 2025, I led the design of the whole app and took on much of the product work.

I interviewed students and teachers and turned their pain points into , designed every flow from onboarding to practice, prioritized the backlog with the CEO and set up the metrics we used to learn from each release.

What happened

Moving the first practice into onboarding took first-practice completion from under 15% to over 80%. Retention stayed close to zero, which showed the core experience wasn’t valuable enough yet and led the business to focus on its established lines.

The constraints

01

A fixed date

We had to launch before September, when the school year started, which meant every feature had to fit a fixed date. App Store review then delayed the launch by almost a month, pushing it past the first weeks of class.

02

No marketing team

We had no dedicated marketing team, only part-time help from another business line, which made it hard to attract leads and reach our download goals. Without enough traffic, it took longer to measure reliably, read real interest and find the students most likely to stay.

03

Limited direction

Direction was limited too. The CEO led the project part-time, and a new project lead who was expected to join halfway through never arrived. In the last three weeks, someone from outside the project took over, with only a few hours a week to give it.

The decisions

  1. 01

    Center the experience on the learning path

    We had to decide what the app was built around: a learning path, or Dino as an AI tutor in a chat. Interviews supported both, since students wanted structured practice and also got stuck with no one to ask.

    We built the experience around the learning path, with the tutor available when students got stuck.

    Our practice model came from learning research: the syllabus was broken into knowledge components, each unlocked after the ones before it. A path showed students where they were and what came next. It also followed the same logic as Duolingo, so we chose a pattern students would already recognize.

    Result

    Students understood how the path worked.

  2. 02

    Put the core experience before growth features

    The business saw a ranking and friend invites as growth engines, with a target of 0.5 referrals per student. We could launch with them or hold them until students valued the core experience.

    I proposed leaving the ranking out of the MVP. It stayed in, since it was cheap to build, and I committed to the decision.

    None of our interviews pointed to social features, and students share an app only once it’s useful to them. At this stage, the priority was building what would make students want to come back.

    Result

    Very few students opened the ranking. Most stayed on the home screen.

  3. 03

    Get students to their first practice sooner

    Over 90% of students finished onboarding, and fewer than 15% of them started a practice. We could keep guiding them from the home screen with stronger cues, or make the first practice the last step of onboarding.

    I moved the first practice into onboarding. Students choose where to start, Dino explains how practice works and how to ask him for help, and the first question appears before the home screen.

    The first station already had an animated halo, and it still depended on students taking the initiative while every other section competed for their attention. Onboarding had a 90% completion rate, so placing practice inside it removed that choice.

    Result

    First-practice completion after onboarding went from under 15% to over 80%. Activation over downloads rose from about 7% to about 36% (target: 25%).

  4. 04

    Go back to discovery to find where the value was

    Activation was above target, and close to zero students came back after the first week. The business asked for a new version of the experience. We could rebuild it around the learning path, rebuild it around the AI tutor, or pause and learn why students weren’t returning.

    I recommended pausing the redesigns to go back to discovery, and the team and the CEO agreed. With no way to reach inactive students, we opened Dino to Ucademy’s current students. I aligned revenue, marketing and customer care, and got a list of about 20 active students to talk to.

    Fixing activation had ruled out onboarding: students reached practice and still left, so the open question was the value of the core experience. Redesigning without users meant guessing again. Ucademy’s students already paid for exam prep and weren’t our target, though they were an audience we could actually reach.

    Result

    They liked the design, and few used Dino regularly. They already had school and Ucademy classes, Dino didn’t add enough on top, and Premium felt expensive.

The outcome

Dino solved activation and didn’t reach retention. The metrics, Ucademy’s students and the teachers we visited pointed the same way: the practice formats didn’t fit every subject, and the experience didn’t give students enough reason to come back. A paywall at the end of onboarding, added to test willingness to pay, cut completion sharply without giving a clear signal.

In November, the business decided that finding product-market fit would take more time and resources than the revenue it could bring, and moved the team to Ucademy’s established lines.

Reflections

01

Fixing activation is what made the real question visible

Once students reached practice and still left, the question moved from onboarding to the value of the core experience.

02

Every feature and experiment needs a hypothesis

Changing something every week is right when you’re looking for product-market fit. We could only see who opened the ranking, an A/B test to collect phone numbers was reverted after a week of work, and the paywall only measured friction. Now I’d agree on a hypothesis, a metric and the decision it informs before building anything.

03

Without a habit, there’s nothing to monetize

The paywall arrived before students had any reason to come back. I’d test willingness to pay only with students who already return on their own.
